# Asthma

Asthma is a long-term condition caused by inflammation of the airways in the lungs.

You can develop asthma at any age.

## Symptoms of asthma

Symptoms include:

- [coughing](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/lungs-and-airways/cough/)
- wheezing
- chest tightness
- [breathlessness](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/lungs-and-airways/shortness-of-breath/)

Symptoms can vary from person to person. They’re often worse at night and early in the morning, or with certain triggers.

Asthma is well controlled in most people most of the time. It shouldn’t restrict your daily life (including your sleep) in any way. You should work with your healthcare professionals to help control your asthma.

## When to get medical advice

### Phone 999 or go to A&E if:

You have asthma and:

- you have severe difficulty breathing – for example, you struggle to speak without pausing, gasping or choking
- you have severe [chest pain](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/cardiovascular-disease/heart-disease/chest-pain/) in the middle of your chest that isn’t going away – it may feel like pressure, tightness or squeezing
- you have pain that spreads to your arms, back, neck and jaw
- you feel more drowsy than usual or find it more difficult to wake up
- your lips or skin are turning pale, blue or grey – on brown or black skin this is easier to see on the palms of your hands

[Information on breathing problems in children](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/lungs-and-airways/breathing-problems-in-children/)

### Contact your GP practice urgently if you:

- are more short of breath than usual, whilst using your inhalers as prescribed
- have pain when breathing in or coughing
- are feeling confused, dizzy, or lightheaded
- have a tight chest that is tighter than usual
- feel sick or are being sick – for example, you can’t keep food or fluids down
- are coughing up blood (haemoptysis)
- have pain or swelling in 1 of your legs
- have [heart palpitations](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/cardiovascular-disease/heart-disease/heart-palpitations/) – this may feel like your heart is racing, going too slowly or skipping a beat or like a fluttering feeling in your chest

If your GP practice is closed, phone 111.

### Speak to your GP practice if you:

- have symptoms that are not improving
- have asthma and your symptoms are affecting your daily life
- have had a [cough](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/lungs-and-airways/cough/) for more than 3 weeks

## What to do if you’re having an asthma attack

1. Take 1 to 2 puffs of your reliever inhaler immediately. Use a spacer if you have one. If it is 2 puffs, these should be 1 at a time.
2. Sit down and try to take slow, steady breaths.
3. If you do not start to feel better, take 1 puff of your reliever inhaler every 1 minute. If you have a blue reliever inhaler, you can take up to 10 puffs. If you have an anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) inhaler or a maintenance and reliever therapy (MART) inhaler, take 1 puff every 1 to 3 minutes. You can take up to 6 puffs.
4. If you do not feel better after taking your inhaler or if you’re worried, phone 999.
5. If an ambulance does not arrive within 10 minutes and you’re still feeling unwell, repeat step 3.

Your inhaler could be empty. Change to another inhaler if you can to check if it helps.

If your symptoms improve and you do not need to phone 999, you still need to see a doctor or asthma nurse within 24 hours.

### What to do after an asthma attack

If you’ve had an asthma attack, you should make an appointment with your GP practice within 48 hours. This will make sure you’re getting the right treatment.

If you have asthma that isn’t being controlled well, talk to your healthcare professional about AIR or MART therapy.

You should make sure that you’re using your inhaler correctly and as prescribed. You should also think about how to reduce your exposure to anything you know that triggers your asthma.

## Diagnosing asthma

If you have symptoms of asthma, your GP may ask you:

- when your symptoms happen
- how often you get symptoms
- if you have noticed any triggers
- if you have [eczema](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/skin/skin-conditions/atopic-eczema/) or [hay fever](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/immune-system/hay-fever/)

### Tests

Tests you may be offered include:

- testing airway inflammation – either with a blood sample or measuring your nitric oxide concentration (a high level can be a sign of airway inflammation)
- spirometry – where you breathe into a machine to check how well your lungs work
- peak expiratory flow test – uses a small hand-held device to measure how fast you can blow air out of your lungs in one breath
- allergy tests – to check if your asthma is linked to allergies like dust mites, pollen, or food
- airways responsiveness – measures how your airways react when they come into contact with a trigger

## Treatment for asthma

There is no cure for asthma but there are treatments that can help:

- prevent future symptoms and attacks
- relieve your symptoms

Most people will take medications once or twice daily, usually using an inhaler. Your nurse, doctor or pharmacist will teach you how to properly use your inhaler.

[Watch Asthma + Lung UK’s videos on how to use your inhaler](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/living-with/inhaler-videos)

Finding and avoiding your possible triggers is also important.

Some [treatments may have side effects](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/conditions/asthma/treatments/concerns).

### Personal asthma action plan

If you’re diagnosed with asthma, your healthcare professional will agree a personal asthma action plan with you. This could include information like:

- the medicines you need to take
- how to recognise when your symptoms are getting worse
- what to do if your symptoms get worse

Your personal asthma action plan should be reviewed with your nurse, doctor or pharmacist at least once a year. If your symptoms are severe, it can be reviewed more regularly.

### Anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) therapy

Anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) therapy involves using a combination inhaler containing 2 types of medicine. These are:

- [steroids](https://www.nhsinform.scot/tests-and-treatments/medicines-and-medical-aids/types-of-medicine/corticosteroids-steroids/) – used to treat inflammation in your airways.
- formoterol – quickly opens up your airways when you have asthma symptoms or an asthma attack

You should only use your AIR inhaler if you experience asthma symptoms. A separate blue reliever inhaler is not needed.

This treatment may be prescribed for children aged 12 and over.

[Asthma + Lung UK has further information on AIR therapy](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/symptoms-tests-treatments/treatments/air)

### Maintenance and reliever therapy (MART)

Maintenance and reliever therapy (MART) is an asthma treatment plan used for preventing and relieving symptoms. It involves using 1 combination inhaler rather than 2 separate preventer and reliever inhalers.

MART inhalers can lower the risk of asthma symptoms and attacks by:

- reducing inflammation in your airways
- preventing symptoms like [breathlessness](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/lungs-and-airways/shortness-of-breath/) and a tight chest
- acting quickly to help with asthma symptoms or an asthma attack
- lowering the risk of an asthma attack or flare-up

You should use your MART inhaler every day to help prevent symptoms. A separate reliever inhaler is not needed.

This treatment may be prescribed for children aged 5 and over.

[Asthma + Lung UK has further information on MART inhalers](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/symptoms-tests-treatments/treatments/mart)

### Separate preventer and reliever inhalers

Preventer inhalers work over time to reduce the inflammation and sensitivity of your airways. They reduce the chances of asthma attacks. They should be used regularly every day as prescribed by your healthcare professional.

Blue reliever inhalers help asthma symptoms quickly. They widen your airways, making it easier to breathe again. The medication will last for around 4 hours.

You should never be given a blue reliever inhaler to use on it’s own. You should always have a preventer inhaler to treat your asthma.

### Used inhalers

There is no recycling scheme available for used inhalers. You should return all your used or expired inhalers to your [community pharmacy](https://www.nhsinform.scot/scotlands-service-directory/pharmacies) for safe disposal. Spacers should be replaced each year if used regularly. Old spacers go into the household bin.

### Inhalers and the environment

Using your preventer inhaler correctly and keeping your symptoms under control is good for your health and the environment. Some inhalers have a lower carbon footprint and are [better for the environment](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/conditions/asthma/how-inhalers-affect-environment) than others. The best inhaler is one that works well for you. Speak to your healthcare professional about whether lower global warming potential inhalers would be right for you.

### Other preventer medicines

If your asthma isn’t controlled by using inhalers alone, you may be prescribed:

- Montelukast – tablets that block part of the chemical reaction involved in the swelling (inflammation) of the airways
- theophyllines – tablets that help widen the airways by relaxing the muscles around them, and are also relatively weak anti-inflammatory agents
- [biological treatments](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/symptoms-tests-treatments/treatments/biologic-therapies)

### Oral steroids

If your asthma is still not under control, you may be prescribed a short course of oral steroid tablets or, more rarely, regular steroid tablets. This treatment should be monitored by a hospital asthma specialist.

Long-term use of oral steroids has serious possible side effects, so they’re only used once other treatments have been tried. Your healthcare professional should talk about the risks and benefits with you. You should be given a steroid treatment card by your healthcare professional.

### Complementary therapies

Breathing exercises can improve symptoms and reduce the need for reliever medicines in some people. These include breathing exercises taught by a physiotherapist and yoga.

[Breathing and relaxation exercises](https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/mental-wellbeing/breathing-and-relaxation-exercises/)

## What causes asthma?

It’s not clear exactly what causes asthma. It’s likely to be a combination of factors.

You’ve a higher chance of having asthma if you have:

- an allergy such as [eczema](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/skin/skin-conditions/atopic-eczema/), [allergic rhinitis](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/ears-nose-and-throat/allergic-rhinitis/), [food allergy](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/nutritional/food-allergy) or [hay fever](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/immune-system/hay-fever)
- a family history of asthma or other similar conditions
- [bronchiolitis](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/conditions/bronchiolitis) (a common childhood lung infection) as a child
- childhood exposure to tobacco smoke, particularly if your mother also smoked during pregnancy
- been born prematurely, especially if you needed a ventilator to support your breathing after birth
- a low birth weight as a result of restricted growth within the womb

Some people may also be at risk of developing asthma through their job.

An asthma trigger is something that irritates your lungs. When you come into contact with a trigger your airways narrow, the muscles around them tighten, and you make more sticky mucus (phlegm).

Common asthma triggers include:

- infections in the upper airways like [colds](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/infections-and-poisoning/common-cold/) and the [flu](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/infections-and-poisoning/flu/)
- dust mites
- animal fur
- feathers
- pollen
- cigarette smoke
- chemical fumes
- pollution
- exercise
- mould or damp
- emotions – including stress or laughing
- [food allergies](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/nutritional/food-allergy)
- certain weather
- [non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)](https://www.nhsinform.scot/tests-and-treatments/medicines-and-medical-aids/types-of-medicine/nsaids/), like [aspirin](https://www.nhsinform.scot/tests-and-treatments/medicines-and-medical-aids/types-of-medicine/aspirin/) and [ibuprofen](https://www.nhsinform.scot/tests-and-treatments/medicines-and-medical-aids/types-of-medicine/ibuprofen/)
- [beta-blockers](https://www.nhsinform.scot/tests-and-treatments/medicines-and-medical-aids/types-of-medicine/beta-blockers/) sometimes given for [high blood pressure](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/cardiovascular-disease/risk-factors-for-cardiovascular-disease/high-blood-pressure-hypertension/) or some types of [heart disease](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/cardiovascular-disease/heart-disease/)
- foods containing sulphites – such as concentrated fruit juice, jam, prawns and many processed or pre-cooked meals

Asthma may also be triggered by [allergens or chemicals breathed in at work](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/conditions/asthma/occupational-asthma). Speak to your GP if you think your symptoms are worse at work and get better on holiday.

You may be able to help your symptoms by avoiding your asthma triggers.

### Exercising with asthma

Speak to your nurse, doctor or pharmacist if you have asthma symptoms during or after exercise. They may review your asthma plan to make sure your symptoms are under control.

They may also advise that you:

- make sure the people you’re exercising with know you have asthma
- increase your fitness levels slowly
- always have your reliever inhaler (usually blue) with you when you exercise
- always warm up and cool down

If you have symptoms while you’re exercising, stop, take your reliever inhaler, and wait until you feel better before starting again.

Read further information about [keeping active](https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/keeping-active/)

### Pregnancy, periods, menopause and asthma

Hormones can change asthma symptoms. For example, around the time of your period, during menopause, and during pregnancy. You should continue to manage your asthma in the same way.

Make sure your asthma is well controlled during pregnancy.

Most medicines used for asthma are safe to take during pregnancy and when breastfeeding. You should not take leukotriene receptor antagonists if you’re pregnant, unless advised to by a healthcare professional.
